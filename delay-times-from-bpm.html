Delay times from BPM: the note-value chart

Every tempo-synced delay is a division of the beat. Once you know the BPM, the milliseconds are arithmetic — and the arithmetic is the difference between a delay that sits in the groove and one that smears it.

Guide · Justin Helmer · Updated 2026-10-20 · 7 min read · delayreverbmixing

The basic formula

One beat in milliseconds is 60,000 ÷ BPM. At 120 BPM, one beat is 500 ms. A quarter-note delay is 500 ms; an eighth-note delay is 250 ms; a sixteenth-note delay is 125 ms. A dotted eighth is 1.5 times the eighth (375 ms at 120), and a triplet is two-thirds of the straight value.

This is the same math a DAW uses when you set a delay to “1/8 dotted” and change the project tempo. Doing it by hand is useful when you are working outside a DAW, matching a hardware pedal, or checking why a plugin’s sync feels wrong.

If you do not want to do the arithmetic, the BPM tool shows delay times for the tempo it detects, including the note values above. The table is still useful when you are working from a known BPM without a file.

Musical warning: a mathematically correct delay can still be wrong for the arrangement. A delay that lands exactly on the grid may fight a swing feel, and a delay that is slightly late may sit better than a perfect eighth. The formula gives you the target; your ears decide whether to use it.

Worked examples

120 BPM: quarter 500 ms, eighth 250 ms, dotted eighth 375 ms, sixteenth 125 ms. A 250 ms ping-pong delay sits on the eighth note; a 375 ms delay pushes against the beat in that familiar dotted-eighth way.

128 BPM: quarter 469 ms, eighth 234 ms, dotted eighth 352 ms, sixteenth 117 ms. This is the house tempo where a 3/16 delay often feels bigger than a straight eighth.

140 BPM: quarter 429 ms, eighth 214 ms, dotted eighth 321 ms, sixteenth 107 ms. At this tempo, short delays start to sound like doubling rather than echo; filter them if the mix gets crowded.

174 BPM: quarter 345 ms, eighth 172 ms, dotted eighth 259 ms, sixteenth 86 ms. Drum-and-bass delays often work best at the eighth or dotted eighth, with the feedback low enough to avoid clutter.

Ping-pong, dotted eighth, and pre-delay

A ping-pong delay alternates left and right. It widens a mono source without making it louder, which is why it is common on vocals and plucks. The timing is still the note value; the stereo pattern is a separate choice.

Reverb pre-delay is not the same as a delay, but the math helps. A short pre-delay (20–60 ms) keeps a vocal from sounding glued to the reverb. A tempo-synced pre-delay can be useful on percussive material, but it is usually better to set it by feel.

Tempo-synced vs manual

If the DAW session has a stable tempo, use tempo sync. If the track drifts, if you are working with live drums, or if you want a delay that deliberately ignores the grid, set the time manually. Manual delays are also useful when a plugin’s sync implementation rounds in a way you do not like.

Common mistakes

  • Using a quarter-note delay on a busy arrangement; it fights the vocal.
  • Setting feedback too high, so the delay outlasts the phrase.
  • Forgetting to high-pass the delay return; low-end repeats muddy the kick.
  • Assuming every genre wants a dotted eighth; sometimes a straight eighth is cleaner.
  • Changing the project tempo after printing a delay without re-checking the time.

FAQ

What is the formula for delay time?

60,000 divided by BPM gives one beat in milliseconds. Divide or multiply for the note value you want.

What is a dotted eighth delay?

An eighth note multiplied by 1.5. At 120 BPM, the eighth is 250 ms, so the dotted eighth is 375 ms.

Should delay be synced to the grid?

For electronic music with a stable tempo, usually yes. For live or drifting material, set it by ear.

Why does my delay sound out of time?

Check whether the plugin is using the project tempo, whether the note value is straight or dotted, and whether the track has a tempo change.

Delay or reverb: which one do you need?

A delay repeats the signal; a reverb simulates a space. If you want a rhythmic answer to a vocal line, use a delay. If you want the source to sit in a room, use a reverb. The note-value table is for the delay; reverb uses time in a different way, although pre-delay can be tempo-aware.

A common combination is a short room reverb for glue and a tempo-synced delay for movement. Put the reverb first in the chain and the delay after it, then high-pass the delay return so the low end does not turn into mud. If the mix already has a lot of sustain, a shorter delay with low feedback will be more useful than a long wash.

A workflow for dialing a delay

  1. Set the note value from the table (start with the eighth or dotted eighth).
  2. Set feedback low — one or two repeats is enough for most vocals.
  3. High-pass the return around 300–500 Hz so it does not crowd the kick.
  4. Automate the send rather than the return; let the delay appear only where the arrangement has space.
  5. Check the mix in mono; a wide ping-pong can disappear or dominate when summed.

If the delay feels late, shorten it by a few milliseconds rather than changing the note value. If it feels cluttered, reduce feedback before reducing the level; the repeats are usually the problem, not the first echo.
